Salvage Protocol · HUD phase 3, section C

Station menu directions

Eight directions across four station menus, plus two takes on the popups. The brief was that these should not look like one another — so the shared vocabulary is deliberately thin: the bevelled plate, the accent cap, the angular cut, and the type roles already settled in the Forged Rig round. Everything inside the plate is per-menu.

Every colour is a live HudKit token rather than an invented one, and every number on screen is a real config value — RefinedOreConfig’s refine ratios and log batch-time curve, ForgeConfig’s tiers and 15-roll pity floor, OreConfig’s tool tiers, CraftingRecipes’ costs. The layouts are sized against content that actually exists.

The craft panel is 640×424 today. Some directions fit that; some need it to grow, which is a real cost and is marked on each.

Forge

Weapons + Smelting tabs · three directions

Your one concrete steer was here: a slot where you put unprocessed ore in, it processes it, and you pick it up. Worth naming a thing that fell out of reading the code — that describes the Smelting tab almost exactly, and Smelting already lives on the Forge station. So direction A treats both tabs as one machine in two modes, which is the strongest reading of the steer. B and C take it somewhere else on purpose, so the choice is real.

A — Crucible 760×520 — panel must grow

What it is

A machine you feed. Left is an input bay showing the cost as loaded material, centre is a chamber that visibly runs, right is an output tray you collect from. Pity becomes heat on the machine body; the Luck Potion becomes a physical additive you slot in.

Why

It makes the Forge a place rather than a list, and it unifies both tabs — Smelting is the same machine in a different mode.

Tradeoff

Three columns at 760px leaves each one narrow. The output tray also implies a roll result that persists until collected — a server change, since ForgeWeapon currently writes the instance straight to the profile. Not just a re-skin.

B — Roll Table 640×424 — fits today

What it is

The Forge as what it mechanically is: a weighted roll. The odds table is the screen, the pity floor is a line drawn across it, and the last five results sit underneath as a streak you can read.

Why

Honest about the system, and it makes Forge tier and Luck Potion legible — buy either and you watch the bars move. Cheapest of the three, and the only Forge direction needing no resize.

Tradeoff

It is a stats screen, not a machine. It answers your ore-in-collect-out steer with a no.

C — Schematic Bench 760×520 — panel must grow

What it is

The weapon drawn as a technical schematic on grid paper, with the rolled affixes as annotation callouts pointing at the part they modify. Families are a left rail; forging is “stamping a new sheet”.

Why

The most distinctive of the three, and it gives affixes somewhere meaningful to live. Scales cleanly — a Legendary with four affixes gets four callouts, not a longer list.

Tradeoff

The most art-dependent. Six weapon families each need their own line drawing or the metaphor collapses into a box with labels.

Smelting

Forge’s second tab · two directions

Both directions exist to surface something the current list hides completely: RefinedOreConfig’s batch time is logarithmic, so a bigger batch is always better per ore. Nothing on screen says so today.

A — Pour Line 760×470 — panel must grow

What it is

Hoppers across the top — all five raw ores with counts and refine ratios — feeding one visible pour. The batch bar is the process; the tray below fills when it lands.

Why

Choice and state on one screen with no navigation, and it is the direction that pairs with Forge A, being the same machine idea.

Tradeoff

Only one batch is visible. If smelting ever runs two jobs, this layout has nowhere to put the second.

B — Batch Dial 640×424 — fits today

What it is

Built around the only decision that matters: how much to melt at once. The dial reads batch time, the slider sets quantity, and the per-ore figure underneath makes the curve visible — 3.4s per ore at 30, 1.4s at 90.

Why

Teaches the mechanic outright, and fits the panel as-is.

Tradeoff

Less atmosphere than the pour line, and the dial is a bigger custom widget than it looks — an arc animating against a server-authoritative finish time.

Welding Station

Robots / Mods / Turrets / Drones · two directions

Welding is about attaching mods to a machine, so the axis here is one rig in depth versus every rig at a glance.

A — Rig Diagram 760×520 — panel must grow

What it is

The robot drawn centre-stage with its three mod slots as hardpoints on leader lines, so “this mod goes here” is spatial rather than a list position.

Why

The only direction whose screen actually says what welding is. An empty third slot reads as a hole in the rig — a far better prompt than a greyed-out row.

Tradeoff

Needs a silhouette per robot, and shows one at a time; comparing two loadouts means clicking back and forth.

B — Loadout Cards 640×424 — fits today

What it is

One card per robot, slots inline as chips, deploy state on the card’s own button. Unbuilt robots are dashed cards showing their cost.

Why

Every robot and every fitted mod visible at once — what you want when deciding what to send out against a wave.

Tradeoff

It is a grid of rows in nicer clothes: the closest of all eight to what exists today, and the least distinct from the other stations.

Workbench

Tools / Auto-Miner / Suit / Base · two directions

The axis here is overview versus detail, and it is really a question about the tabs: A deletes them, B keeps them and makes each one earn its click.

A — Progression Rails 640×424 — fits today

What it is

No tabs. All four tracks are rails with your position marked, the next step named, and the cost underneath. Affordable steps get the accent button; unaffordable ones say what you are short.

Why

The Workbench is one question — how upgraded am I — and tabs hide three quarters of the answer behind clicks. Seeing all four costs together is also how you decide what to mine next.

Tradeoff

Dropping tabs means StationConfig.Types.Crafting.Tabs stops describing this station, and the Base tab’s turret placement has nowhere to go.

B — Spec Sheet 640×424 — fits today

What it is

Tabs stay, but each becomes a page about one thing: the equipped item as a hero with real stats, what it still cannot do, its fitted mods, then an explicit before/after for the next tier.

Why

The before/after row is the piece missing today — the current list states a cost but never states what you get. Also the only Workbench direction with room for turret placement.

Tradeoff

Four clicks to see four numbers. The overview A gives for free costs navigation here.

Popups

Toast, confirm, mod picker, case opening · two directions

Included in this round rather than retrofitted, since a popup that does not match its parent is more jarring than one that matches nothing. The axis is whether a popup belongs to the panel that raised it or interrupts everything.

A — Docked

What it is

Every popup borrows the open panel’s edges: the toast hangs off its accent cap, a confirmation replaces the footer, the mod picker slides out of the right edge, a case opens inside the panel body.

Why

Nothing floats unattached, so a popup always tells you which menu raised it — and it inherits whichever menu direction wins instead of being a fifth style.

Tradeoff

Each parent panel has to reserve the space, and a popup raised from the world rather than a menu has no frame to dock to.

B — Lifted Slabs

What it is

Each popup is its own plate on a scrim, with a shadow and its own accent cap — red for a destructive confirm, orange for a choice, gold for a reveal.

Why

Unmissable and consistent regardless of what raised it. The case opening in particular wants a full takeover.

Tradeoff

The scrim interrupts. Right for a confirm, wrong for a rejection toast firing mid-wave — so realistically the toast wants A’s treatment even if everything else takes B’s.

What I need from you to converge

  1. Forge: A, B or C. This is the one real fork. A is the literal reading of your steer and costs a server change; B is cheap and fits today; C is the most distinctive and costs six line drawings.
  2. Is the panel allowed to grow past 640×424? Four of the ten directions need it. If the answer is no, the set collapses to B / Batch Dial / Loadout Cards / either Workbench — a coherent but much tamer round.
  3. Workbench: keep the tabs or not. Rails deletes them, which contradicts StationConfig as written. Worth deciding before either gets built.
  4. Popups: one treatment or a split. My recommendation is the split — docked toast, lifted everything else — but that is two mechanisms to maintain instead of one.
